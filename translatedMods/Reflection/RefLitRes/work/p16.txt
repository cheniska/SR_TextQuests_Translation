151	Illness and Enlightenment
152	"Mysterious luatation" — that was the diagnosis the examination produced, and the gaal woman standing by the apparatus smiled apologetically as she took the readings.<br>— Your condition is satisfactory. There is marked overexertion, slowed reactions, increased sensitivity to light and slight mental deviations. But that is normal for people like you.<br>— Like what? — the patient asked in bewilderment. Lately he had become worse at taking in information and kept drifting in the clouds, pondering the sublime. Of course, for the gaal race this was nothing unusual; to some extent their present civilization had been formed on this, having recovered by hard work from the first devastating wars and afterward almost completely renouncing violence.<br>The doctor laughed slightly, but fell silent at once. In her belly the disturbed child stirred discontentedly, due to be born soon.
153	The door slammed shut, and the humanoid tried to find the address he needed. It was getting dark fast outside and starting to turn cold. But luckily the settlement was small, and the newcomer reached his destination without incident.<br>After knocking on the door, he heard approaching footsteps and the buzzing of an old-model scanning device. A camera rolled out, and Raamis said politely:<br>— Hello!<br>But in reply the stunned ranger heard:<br>— Go away! I'm not joining your sect and I don't need to learn Enlightenment either. Go to Gaaldok and fool your fellow countrymen, you frauds!<br>— Excuse me? — he asked, seeing a young girl appear on the screen, who, raising her eyes to the monitor, immediately said in embarrassment:<br>— Oh, you're not one of the Witnesses of Erigoof, sorry. It's just that they're sick of coming to me every day.<br>The door opened, and the gaal saw a modestly dressed girl looking him over in bewilderment.
154	— A ranger? But how did you end up here? Usually you don't go farther than the capital.<br>— I'll tell you everything later, but for now I want to rent a room from you. I'll pay well and won't cause any trouble.<br>— Come in, since you came, and I could really use the money.<br>The alien had to duck a little so as not to hit the doorway, low for him, and with a polite bow he went inside. The place did not shine with luxury, but it was quite fit to live in, and even comfortable.<br>— You have a nice house, Miss Anders, — Raamis said appraisingly, taking off his jacket and remaining in his furnas — the native analogue of a T-shirt, more like a wide shirt.<br>— That's true, — the girl answered, and down to business: — How long do you want to stay?<br>— About a week, if possible.<br> — If you pay a thousand credits, the room will be at your disposal.<br>The gaal opened his eyes wide in surprise. So expensive to rent, and far from the city too. It was sheer madness, especially since after the trip and paying in advance for parking, he had no more than fifteen hundred left on hand.
155	— Maybe we can agree on five hundred at least? — Raamis asked, looking at his new acquaintance pleadingly.<br>— Seven hundred fifty, and not a credit less, — the mistress of the house answered, — and only because you're a decent alien and not some lout from Shukheo or thug from Samtaru. Of course, there are scoundrels among your kind too, but I think you're not one of them. Befriending our Granny Martha — no alien has ever managed that, and she doesn't much like talking to other people either.<br>— All right, I agree, — the ranger exhaled after thinking it over. On the ship there was also a bank account blocked for the duration of his stay, so the humanoid wouldn't starve. For the time being... (a fragment of a phrase merged with a line) the neighbor is quite a nice woman. She's the one who helped me get here.<br>— That's great, and for now you can take a bath, your room is on the left, so make yourself at home.
156	Rebecca withdrew to her room, and the gaal decided to look over his temporary lodging. The small but tastefully arranged room held a minimum of items, but for some reason one wanted to stay here longer than a week, and the neatly made bed beckoned, though Raamis did not like this kind of sleeping place. Still, he liked the familiar tuun-ga better, which helped the blood rush to the brain overnight and let him wake in the morning with fresh thoughts. True, not everyone understood how one could sleep vertically, and feet up besides, but every race had its peculiarities.<br>After talking with the hostess and washing up peacefully in the shower, the newcomer wished the hostess good night and lay down on the bed. Sleep would not come at all, and sitting by the window, he looked at the starry sky peeking out through the gray smoke. Tonight was clear, and he wanted to give himself over to abstract thoughts and, picking some local flowers, assemble an Iike-Baanu, but restraining himself, he took an old DND player out of his bag and played at minimum volume one of the faeyan films recorded in its memory.<br>The movie turned out to be rather dull, but not without a peculiar sense. Although Raamis had trouble telling apart some dialects, he understood what was going on. A blaster shot rang out. A bandit on screen was threatening a frightened hostage with something, and the latter, using a pocket multiresonator, managed to free himself and finished off the surprised raider. Almost like human blockbusters, only with the faeyan love of technology and the relative brevity of the films. It was already getting light outside, and the gaal decided to doze a little after all.
157	He woke up a couple of hours later. The mistress of the apartment had turned on the stereovision, which was showing a report from the Regts system. The announcer said that pirates, in company with some defector ranger, had freed the system from dominators but had not handed it over to the Coalition forces. Soon the channel was switched, and the gaal found himself standing dressed in the living room.<br>— Good morning, — the girl said with slight drowsiness. — You got up early. You could have kept sleeping.<br>— I don't feel like it. Insomnia has gotten to me.<br>— Well, it's nothing, it'll all pass soon. It must be interesting to be a ranger, right, Zaruoon Raamis Ga?<br>— How do you know? — the newcomer asked in surprise. — I never introduced myself by my full name?<br>— You dropped your passport out of your bag! — Rebecca Anders answered good-naturedly, — Good thing it was still on my doorstep and not somewhere in the street. Otherwise you'd have been stuck here for at least two months restoring your documents. The Absent-minded Man from Basseynaya Street.<br>The gaal smiled slightly and thanked the girl, taking his document from her. Evidently the aftereffects of the luatation were still making themselves felt. Especially since no one had attacked him yet and everyone he met had been quite friendly. It was good that he had listened to the doctor and decided to rest calmly for a while.<br>The first three days passed without adventures, only occasionally the newcomer began to notice curious glances on himself, and Rebecca, who had seemed rude and taciturn at first, turned out to be a quite sweet girl, though an alien. She always smiled at him when they met, invited him to the table when she was about to eat, and looked strangely when her tenant greeted other women. They talked a lot, went for walks in the forest and sat for long periods on the bank of a small river that had recently thawed.
158	Once, knocking on his room, she saw the DND player and soon disappeared, returning from the pantry with a stack of old discs. Raising his head in surprise, Raamis looked at the covers: ancient and relatively new human films, reissued in this format. It was a real surprise compared to the already tiresome faeyan cinema, which he had managed to watch to death since buying the device.<br>— I've wanted to watch these for a long time, can I run them on your player? I'll take it and return it in the morning.<br>The gaal paused the film. The faeyan froze in a threatening pose, then vanished from the screen.<br>— Stay here, — he told his housemate. — We'll watch together, I like human films too.<br>— And I thought you considered them stupid and meaningless, — the girl said with a slight jab. — Good that it isn't so.<br>— They're quite interesting. And they have a right to exist.<br>Rebecca sat down on the edge of the bed. They watched without looking away almost all night. She — with extraordinary, almost childlike delight and a sense of nostalgia, he — with undisguised interest and curiosity. But soon, unable to withstand the fatigue, both fell asleep unnoticed while the film's brave hero dealt with a monstrous dragon.
159	— It seems we underestimated your ancient ancestors, — Raamis began. — They were wiser than you think. They predicted the meeting with the faeyans, klissans, bots, the appearance of the dominators and even the look of Terron and Keller, albeit with small inaccuracies. I always proved to my colleagues that humans are a unique race.<br>The girl said nothing, she did not say that almost all of it was fiction, mistaken by some for truth. And in this case, for mysterious messages. Although humans once also took crop circles and various signs for alien warnings, though, as it turned out later, their meaning was equal to the inscription "Vasya was here" on some fence. A small pause fell and Anders decided to change the subject. The title of a unique race from an alien had flattered her too much.<br>— Colleagues? At the Rangers' Center?<br>— No, before the war, when I worked at the Archive for the Study of Alien Civilizations.<br>— So you're a scientist? But why did you join the rangers then, and not that same science or military base?
160	— You know, I thought I'd be more useful to my people this way, especially since I'm very weak in robotics, so I'd better break enemy equipment than my own.<br>Both laughed, and the gaal sadly realized that soon he would set off on a flight again and leave this hospitable place and the pleasant hostess of a rented home, but a home nonetheless. He wanted to stay longer, but the war went on and the dominators might fly to Antares too. So the day after tomorrow he had to be at the spaceport and keep fighting. So that the nasty robots would not dare to reach here and destroy this pleasant corner.<br>Still, five days later, he did not regret paying so much money, which had seemed a large sum at first. It was worth it. And if things worked out, he would come back here once more. And then. Then, perhaps, he would stay here forever or else take Rebecca, with whom they had become good friends in this time, to Gaaldok. Because there was room for everyone there, regardless of race and sex. And simply because he wanted to be with her longer.
